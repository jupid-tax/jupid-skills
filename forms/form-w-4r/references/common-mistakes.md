# Common Form W-4R mistakes — and how to avoid them

Form W-4R has only a few fields, but recipients still make consequential mistakes. The penalties for under-withholding (§6654 estimated-tax penalty) or for missing the §72(t) early-withdrawal tax (separate from W-4R) can add up quickly.

This reference covers the top mistakes recipients make, the symptoms, and the fixes. Rules verified 2026-10-06 against the 2026 Form W-4R, Pub. 505 (2026), Pub. 575 (2025), Pub. 15 (2026) and the 2025 Instructions for Form 5329.

---

## 1. Choosing the wrong form (W-4R vs. W-4P vs. W-4)

**The mistake**: Recipient files W-4R when the payment is periodic (installments at regular intervals over more than one year), or files W-4P for a one-time lump sum, or uses W-4R for wages such as severance pay.

**Why it happens**: Form W-4R took over nonperiodic payments and eligible rollover distributions from Form W-4P (the 2026 W-4R's Privacy Act notice still mentions "a previous Form W-4P that you completed with respect to your nonperiodic payments"). Many recipients still think of "withholding on retirement distributions" as a single concept, and some think any lump sum from a former employer is a W-4R payment.

**Symptom**:
- W-4R given for periodic payments → the payer can't use it for those payments; without a W-4P, periodic payments are withheld as if Single with no adjustments (Pub. 575 (2025)).
- W-4P given for a lump sum → the payer applies the nonperiodic default (10%) or the ERD 20%.
- W-4R given for severance → not applicable; severance is wages (Pub. 15 (2026)).

**Fix**: Identify the right form. Periodic (more than one year of scheduled installments, other than IRA distributions payable on demand) → W-4P. Nonperiodic or ERD → W-4R. Wages, bonuses, severance → W-4.

**Prevention**: Before signing, confirm the payment structure with the payer. "Is this a one-time distribution or recurring?" and "Is this paid from a retirement plan, IRA, or annuity, or through payroll?" are the key questions.

## 2. Under-withholding a lump sum and getting hit with §6654 estimated-tax penalty

**The mistake**: Recipient takes a $100,000 IRA lump sum, elects 0% withholding via W-4R, and assumes they'll pay the tax at filing. The IRS imposes a §6654 underpayment penalty because total withholding + estimated tax falls short of the safe harbor.

**Why it happens**: Recipient confuses "I'll pay the tax at filing" with "no penalty." The §6654 safe harbor requires that total tax payments during the year (withholding + estimated taxes) equal at least:

- 90% of the current year's tax liability, OR
- 100% of the prior year's tax (110% if prior-year AGI > $150,000; $75,000 MFS)

(No penalty if the balance due after withholding and credits is under $1,000; Pub. 505 (2026), chapter 2.) If neither safe harbor is met, the §6654 penalty applies on the underpayment at the IRC §6621 underpayment rate (federal short-term rate plus 3 percentage points).

**Symptom**: Notice from the IRS in spring (after filing) assessing the underpayment penalty. Or, if the recipient self-computes via Form 2210, they discover the penalty.

**Fix**: 
- **For the current year**: Pay an additional Form 1040-ES estimated payment ASAP, or have more withheld (a new W-4R for a later distribution, or extra W-4 withholding). Withholding counts as paid evenly through the year unless the taxpayer elects actual dates, so extra withholding late in the year can cover earlier quarters (Pub. 505 (2026), chapter 2).
- **For prior years**: The penalty is part of the tax bill. Pay it.

**Prevention**: Before electing 0% withholding on a large distribution, run a back-of-envelope calculation:

- Use the form's Marginal Rate Tables (two-step method) or a projection to estimate the tax on the distribution
- Compare to current withholding (W-2, W-4P, etc.) — is the additional tax already covered?
- If not, either elect a non-zero W-4R rate OR pay quarterly estimates

## 3. Not filing W-4R when required (defaults apply)

**The mistake**: Recipient doesn't submit W-4R to the payer for an IRA distribution or 401(k) lump sum. The payer applies the default withholding rate (10% for other nonperiodic, 20% for ERD). (An earlier election on file for the same plan or IRA generally carries over; for payments that began before 2026 the current election remains in effect; form, pages 1–2.)

**Why it happens**: Recipient assumes "I don't need to fill anything; the payer will figure it out." Technically true (defaults apply), but the recipient may have wanted a different rate.

**Symptom**: Distribution comes through with default withholding. If the recipient wanted 0% (for a Roth conversion, for example, where they want full conversion amount), they got 10% withheld and lost the benefit.

**Fix**: 
- For the current distribution: probably too late — payer processes the distribution at the default rate, sends out the net, files 1099-R.
- For future distributions: file W-4R BEFORE the next distribution is processed.

**Prevention**: For any large or unusual distribution, file W-4R explicitly. Don't rely on defaults. The form takes 5 minutes; the wrong default can cost thousands.

## 4. Choosing wrong form: W-4R vs. W-4P specifically

**The mistake**: A retiree receiving a monthly pension submits W-4R to the pension payer to change withholding on the monthly checks. The form doesn't apply — the pension is periodic.

**Why it happens**: Two separate forms cover retirement payments. Recipients submit the wrong one.

**Symptom**: Payer keeps the existing periodic election (or, if none, withholds as if Single with no adjustments in Steps 2–4 of Form W-4P; Pub. 575 (2025)).

**Fix**: Submit W-4P (or the payer's substitute form). For periodic pension benefits, W-4P is the correct form. Some payers (e.g., federal retirement systems) use their own online or substitute forms; ask the payer.

**Prevention**: Before submitting any retirement-distribution withholding form, confirm with the payer: "Periodic or nonperiodic?"

## 5. Forgetting the §72(t) additional tax on early distributions

**The mistake**: Recipient under 59½ takes a $40,000 IRA distribution, elects 22% withholding via W-4R, and assumes 22% covers everything. At tax time, they owe an additional $4,000 (10% of $40,000) under §72(t) because they're under 59½ and no exception applies.

**Why it happens**: The W-4R default rates and the form's Marginal Rate Tables cover regular income tax only. The §72(t) 10% is an additional tax reported on Schedule 2 (Form 1040) line 8, with Form 5329 when required (2025 Schedule 2; 2025 Instructions for Form 5329).

**Symptom**: Unexpectedly large tax bill at filing, or §6654 underpayment penalty if quarterly estimates weren't made.

**Fix**:
- For the current year: report the additional tax on Schedule 2 line 8 (Form 5329 if required) and pay it with the return; consider an estimated payment now to limit the penalty.
- For future early withdrawals: factor §72(t) into the rate decision (table rate + 10 points).

**Prevention**: For under-59½ withdrawals, ask the user whether to set line 2 at (table rate + 10) OR pay estimated tax for the §72(t) part. Check the exceptions in the Instructions for Form 5329 (e.g., 01 separation from service in or after the year of reaching 55 — plans only; 02 substantially equal periodic payments; 03 disability; 05 medical expenses over 7.5% of AGI; 06 QDRO alternate payee — plans only; 08 higher education — IRAs only; 09 first home up to $10,000 — IRAs only; 19 birth or adoption up to $5,000; 23 emergency personal expense). Hardship is NOT an exception. Ask which exception applies; never assume one.

## 6. Trying to get below 20% on an ERD

**The mistake**: Recipient takes a $50,000 401(k) lump sum after leaving employer. Wants 0% withholding because they intend to roll the full amount to an IRA within 60 days. Submits W-4R with Line 2 = "0".

**Why it happens**: Recipient confuses ERD rules (20% mandatory) with other nonperiodic rules (0–100%).

**Symptom**: Payer applies 20% under §3405(c) regardless of the W-4R ("You can't choose withholding at a rate of less than 20% (including '-0-')"). Recipient receives $40,000 net. To roll over the full $50,000 within 60 days, the recipient must add $10,000 from other funds; otherwise only $40,000 is rolled and the $10,000 withheld is a taxable distribution, possibly also subject to the §72(t) 10% (Pub. 575 (2025), "Rolling over more than amount received").

**Fix**: 
- Better path: do a **direct trustee-to-trustee rollover** instead of indirect rollover. Direct rollover bypasses the 20% withholding entirely (Form 1099-R Code G, $0 withholding).
- If indirect rollover already happened: contribute the $10,000 from non-IRA cash within 60 days to make the rollover whole. The withheld $10,000 is recovered when filing Form 1040 (it becomes federal tax payment / refund).

**Prevention**: Before taking an ERD, confirm with the payer whether you want direct or indirect rollover. Direct is almost always better when the goal is to move funds between retirement accounts.

## 7. Mismatched name/SSN/address

**The mistake**: Recipient's W-4R has a different name, SSN, or address than the payer's records. The payer rejects the form.

**Why it happens**: Recipient moved, married/divorced (name change), or made a typo on the SSN.

**Symptom**: Payer's distribution-processing team flags the form. Distribution is delayed.

**Fix**: 
- Update the payer's records first (via the payer's address change form, name change form, etc.)
- Then resubmit W-4R with the matching information

**Prevention**: Pull the most recent statement from the payer and copy the name/SSN/address verbatim onto W-4R.

## 8. Submitting W-4R to the IRS instead of the payer

**The mistake**: Recipient mails W-4R to the IRS along with their tax return.

**Why it happens**: Recipient confuses W-4R with other tax forms that get filed with IRS.

**Symptom**: IRS receives a stray form with no return; either ignores or returns to recipient. The payer never receives the form. Default withholding applies to the distribution.

**Fix**: Resubmit W-4R to the payer (IRA custodian, plan administrator, employer).

**Prevention**: W-4R is **delivered to the payer**, not filed with the IRS. The form says: "Give Form W-4R to the payer of your retirement payments."

---

## 9. Not signing or not dating the form

**The mistake**: Recipient fills the form but forgets to sign or date it.

**Why it happens**: Speed; e-signature confusion; fillable PDF without signature field activated.

**Symptom**: "This form is not valid unless you sign it." The payer returns the form or applies the default rate. Distribution is delayed.

**Fix**: Sign and date, resubmit.

**Prevention**: Before submitting, verify all fields including signature and date are completed.

---

## 10. Using last year's revision

**The mistake**: Recipient pulls W-4R from an old saved file and submits to payer. The payer accepts but the form revision is outdated.

**Why it happens**: IRS occasionally revises W-4R (rates, instructions). Recipient uses cached version.

**Symptom**: The payer may still accept it, but the Marginal Rate Tables on page 1 change every year (2026 thresholds = 2026 bracket starts + 2026 standard deduction), so a rate picked from an old form's tables can be wrong.

**Fix**: Re-pull the current version from https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/fw4r.pdf and re-submit.

**Prevention**: Always pull from the IRS site in the year of the distribution. The URL is stable; the year printed in the top-right corner of page 1 ("2026") and the footer "Form W-4R (2026)" identify the revision.

## 11. Conflating Roth conversion withholding with regular distribution

**The mistake**: Recipient is doing a Roth conversion (Traditional IRA → Roth IRA) and elects 22% withholding via W-4R, thinking the 22% will satisfy the tax on the conversion.

**Why it happens**: Recipient understands the conversion is taxable and wants to "pay the tax."

**Symptom**: The 22% withheld from the traditional IRA is not converted, so the Roth receives less. If the recipient is under 59½, the withheld amount is a distribution that can also be subject to the §72(t) 10% additional tax (Pub. 590-B).

**Fix**:
- For future conversions: ask whether the user wants "-0-" on line 2 and to pay the conversion tax from other funds with Form 1040-ES; this keeps the full amount in the Roth.
- For a completed conversion: a Roth conversion can't be recharacterized for conversions made after 2017 (IRC §408A(d)(6)(B)(iii), added by P.L. 115-97).

**Prevention**: For Roth conversions, raise the withholding-vs-estimated-tax choice before the request is submitted; don't pick for the user.

## Summary checklist

Before signing W-4R:

- [ ] Right form (W-4R, not W-4P, and not W-4 for wages or severance)
- [ ] Distribution classification confirmed (ERD vs. other nonperiodic)
- [ ] Rate within allowed range (≥ 20% for ERD; 0–100% for other; ≥ 10% if delivered outside the U.S.)
- [ ] Recipient under 59½? Factor the §72(t) 10% additional tax (Schedule 2 line 8; not in the default rates)
- [ ] Other income / withholding considered for §6654 safe harbor
- [ ] Name / SSN / address match payer records
- [ ] Form delivered to payer (NOT to IRS)
- [ ] Signed and dated
- [ ] Current year's IRS revision used
- [ ] State withholding handled separately
- [ ] Roth conversion → ask: withholding (reduces the amount converted) or estimated tax from other funds
